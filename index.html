“There are countless stories in the world that we have yet to hear, and countless others we may never have had the chance to hear.”

I believe in the power of visual storytelling to move people through images. Behind every image, there is a reason, where the camera belongs, how light should feel, and how each visual choice serves the story.

This is how I approach every project. I begin with the question of why, searching for the visual language that allows each story to be felt and understood.

I made my debut as a cinematographer with a feature film I shot during my senior year of university, which was released theatrically and later on Netflix Korea, making me the youngest cinematographer to make a feature film debut in South Korea. I am currently based in Vancouver, Canada, and am a member of the Canadian Society of Cinematographers (CSC).

SYNOPSIS

“May my beloved child always be bright and healthy.”

Two fathers find themselves unable to live with their children after divorce. Having left their families for different reasons, they should be able to see their children through their legally guaranteed right to visitation and contact. Yet the reality is far from simple. Amid appointments, waiting, and repeated conflicts, both fathers struggle to reclaim their right to see their children.

Following their stories, the film examines the invisible barriers between custodial and non-custodial parents in divorced families. Why does a legally guaranteed right to visitation and contact fail to function in reality? And amid social prejudice toward non-custodial parents and the limitations of the system, what kind of pain and confusion do parental conflicts ultimately leave on children?

SYNOPSIS

“Dad, don’t erase it. That’s your real face.”

At night, single father Jung Seok becomes a drag queen under dazzling lights, embracing the person he truly is. But fearing that society’s prejudice might hurt his eight year old daughter Eun Soo, he gives up drag and hides behind the role of a “normal father.” Eun Soo, however, sees no contradiction in her father. To her, he is simply the most sparkling dad in the world. Determined to bring back the part of him he has erased, she begins a reckless and mischievous mission, even stealing and reselling cosmetics to help him return to the stage.

EYE looks at the world through a child’s eyes and questions the boundaries adults impose in the name of normality and protecting children. Through the relationship between Jung Seok and Eun Soo, the film asks whether the desire to protect children has instead become a reason to erase difference. Ultimately, it suggests that family is not defined by gender, convention, or social expectations, but by the courage to accept someone exactly as they are.

SYNOPSIS

A group of students visits the home of a renowned elderly writer, and through poetry, they gradually grow closer. One day, however, one of the students misunderstands something and runs away with the writer’s manuscript.

What are we misunderstanding as we go through life?

At least once in a lifetime, anyone can make an irreversible mistake and find themselves having to say goodbye to a beautiful memory, just like the boy in the film. Which character do you find yourself most deeply immersed in?

And in the final scene, when the writer finally acknowledges the truth about the manuscript the boy stole, what did the boy truly feel?

SYNOPSIS

In the sterile, blindingly white streets of Vancouver, Soo, a struggling Korean immigrant actress, survives on the fringes of society. After a humiliating encounter at her barista job, she receives a bizarre offer from Margaret, a cold matriarch of the city's elite: to become the living replacement for Margaret’s deceased daughter, Emma.

There is no script—only a set of rigorous "parameters" defining Emma’s personality, habits, and tastes. Desperate for salvation from her decaying reality, Soo accepts the role, treating it as the ultimate method-acting challenge. She performs her (Emma) identity, meticulously erasing "Soo" to inhabit the hollowed-out image of the perfect daughter.

As Soo masters the performance, the boundaries between the actress and the deceased girl blur. She finds a haunting sense of belonging in this artificial paradise, until the "settings" begin to demand the total erasure of her subjectivity. Under Margaret's chilling corrections, Soo realizes that in this utopia designed by someone else, any trace of her true self is a glitch that must be violently deleted.
